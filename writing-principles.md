# Writing Principles

Rules to follow when drafting/editing content for "The Ultimate Guide to Agentic Coding." Add to this file as more come up.

1. **Write in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English.** Added 2026-08-27; promoted to rule 1 on 2026-09-02. The concrete constraints, in priority order:
    - **One idea per sentence.** Descriptive sentences max 25 words; instructional/procedural sentences max 20 words. One instruction per sentence.
    - **Active voice, always.** "The agent reads the file," not "the file is read by the agent."
    - **Simple tenses only** (simple past, simple present, simple future). Avoid perfect and progressive forms where a simple tense carries the same meaning.
    - **Avoid `-ing` forms** used as nouns or as verbs of a clause. Prefer "when the agent runs" over "when running the agent." Established terms keep their `-ing` (e.g. "context engineering," "vibe coding," "prompt engineering," "sandboxing") because they are the names of the things.
    - **One word, one meaning; one meaning, one word.** Pick a term for a concept and reuse it for the whole guide. Do not alternate between synonyms for variety.
    - **Noun clusters max 3 words.** Break "agent context window management" into a phrase with a preposition.
    - **Keep articles and relative pronouns.** Do not delete "the," "a," or "that" to shorten a sentence.
    - **Write what to do, not what to avoid,** unless the warning is the point.
    - **Paragraphs max 6 sentences** (rule 17/29's paragraph limits already hold us far tighter, so this rarely binds).
    - **Exempt from rewriting:** verbatim quotes, source and article titles, proper nouns, and official product/feature names. STE governs our own prose only. Never restyle a person's quoted words to fit it, and never let it override the quote-fidelity rules (4, 22, 25) or the source-fidelity rule.
    - **Not exempt but constrained:** lesson headings still follow rule 31's "How To [verb] X" Title Case template. Where rule 31 and STE conflict on a heading, rule 31 wins.
2. **No em dashes.** Replace with a period and a new sentence, a semicolon, or "and." Never an em dash.
3. **Never write "This matters to you" or "Why this matters" (or close variants).** Say what matters directly instead of flagging that you're about to say it.
4. **Reserve blockquote-style quotes for top AI people (named individuals), not for docs/guides.** When citing a docs page (e.g. Claude Code Best Practices), fold the source's point into regular prose with an inline link instead of a blockquote with an attribution line.
5. **Don't tack citation links on as a trailing parenthetical.** Weave the link into the main sentence itself (e.g. "Anthropic's analysis of ~400,000 real Claude Code sessions" as the linked phrase) rather than writing the sentence plain and appending "(Title, date)" or "(url)" afterward. Reads more natural and crisp.
6. **Don't add a per-lesson `**Source:**` line or an end-of-module `## Further Reading` list.** Superseded 2026-08-06 (Module 4 finished-version comparison): the user's actual published Module 4 dropped both entirely, replacing them with attribution woven inline into sentences throughout (per rules 5/22). An earlier version of this rule (corrected 2026-08-04) said to keep both, based on Module 1 and Module 3 as they stood then; the Module 4 edit pass shows the real current pattern moved away from footer/list citations altogether. Attribute inline as you draft; don't append a citation block or list at any level.
7. **Every module opens with a 1-2 line expectation-setting statement, then a module index, before lesson 1.1 starts.** The opening line should give the reader full clarity on what the module covers; the index lists every lesson (linked) so readers can see the shape of the module before diving in.
8. **Teach the general concept before the Claude Code-specific implementation.** When a lesson covers something like "tools" or "memory," open with a short explanation of what that concept means in agentic coding broadly, then move into how Claude Code specifically implements it. Concept first, tool second, so readers build transferable understanding, not just Claude Code trivia.
9. **Only name "Claude Code" (or "Claude") when saying something specific to it.** For general/universal statements about agentic coding, say "agentic coding tool," "the model," "an agent," etc. Don't tack on "Claude Code included" or similar asides to a generic claim. Reserve the Claude Code name for: Claude Code-specific features/behavior, "Try This in Claude Code" hands-on steps, and quotes/data explicitly about Claude Code.
10. **Checklists go in a code block (plain text, not markdown checkboxes/bold).** Drop the `**[Checklist] ... :**` label styling and `- [ ]` list syntax; put the checklist inside a fenced code block instead, so it visually reads and copies like the other code/command blocks in the guide. Doesn't need to be functional/copy-ready until the site build; just needs to look like a code block for now.
11. **Hands-on step heading is "Try This in Claude Code," not "Do it with me."** We write as a brand/team, not first-person singular, so "me" is wrong; this heading is literal and descriptive about what the reader is about to do.
12. **Don't use "worth" (e.g. "worth naming," "worth noting," "worth calling out").** Reads as an AI-generated tic. Just state the thing directly instead of flagging that it's worth stating.
13. **Every heading, at any level, must stand on its own and be specific/benefit-oriented, not just punchy in an index.** Applies to subheadings inside a lesson, not just lesson-level titles (clarified 2026-08-04, Module 4) — a subheading like "Five Principles, Applied" fails the same test as a vague lesson title. Avoid vague pronouns ("Giving It Tools") or headline-only phrasing that assumes surrounding context; a reader landing on the heading mid-page should immediately know what they get from it (e.g. "Give Your Agent File, Shell, and Search Tools" instead of "Giving It Tools").
14. **Cross-references to other lessons link by concept/description, never by number or module name (e.g. not "from 1.3," not "see 2.4," not "Module 1 covered X" or "from Module 1").** Superseded 2026-07-28: an earlier version of this rule said to also name the module ("Module 1's permissions and sandboxing lesson"); dropped that. Readers remember "the plan-act-observe-verify loop" or "context engineering," not section numbers or module numbers, so just link the descriptive phrase itself to the anchor, e.g. "the [plan → act → observe → verify loop](module-1-fundamentals.md#13-...) we covered earlier" instead of "the agent loop from [1.3](...)" or "...from Module 1." Use phrasing like "we covered," "covered earlier," or "the lesson on X" instead of a module reference.
15. **When a sentence maps multiple parallel items to concepts (e.g. "this call is X; this block is Y; this check is Z"), use a bulleted list instead of a single run-on sentence with semicolons.** Easier to scan than a dense inline mapping.
16. **Every hands-on example task should look like "the agent doing coding things," not a generic/non-coding stand-in (e.g. arithmetic, trivia).** Even a zero-risk smoke test with no tools should use a code-flavored prompt (e.g. "write a one-line function that reverses a string") instead of something like "what is 17 * 24," so the exercise reinforces the module's framing from its first line instead of feeling disconnected from it.
17. **Every hands-on section ("Build This Yourself" / "Try This in Claude Code") follows the same Goal → numbered Steps → Expected Result template, no exceptions, applied retroactively to existing modules too.**
    - **Goal:** one line, stated before the steps, saying what this exercise proves/builds.
    - **Steps:** numbered list, one action per step, each with its own code block where needed (don't bury multiple actions inside one paragraph).
    - **Expected result:** one line at the end stating exactly what the reader should see, so they can confirm success without interpreting prose.
    - Purpose: reader should be able to execute top-to-bottom without re-reading or parsing paragraphs — think less, do more.
18. **Paragraphs max out at 2-3 sentences, AND must look short, not just count short.** A paragraph with 3 dense/long sentences (compound clauses, semicolons, parentheticals) can still wrap to 4-5 lines on screen and read as a wall of text even though it passes the sentence-count check. When a paragraph wraps past ~3 lines on a typical screen, split it further or tighten the sentences themselves, even down to a single sentence per paragraph if needed. Judge by how it looks rendered, not just by counting periods.
19. **The first time a named third-party tool, product, or framework is mentioned (e.g. LangChain, CrewAI, pytest, git), link it to its official docs/homepage.** Readers may not know what it is; a link gives them a way to look it up without breaking the sentence's flow. Don't over-link repeat mentions of the same name later in the same lesson.
20. **The "End of Module" closing summary lists what the reader should have accomplished as bullets, not an inline comma-separated sentence.** Keep the "By this point you should have:" lead-in and the transition line to the next module as prose, but put each accomplishment on its own bullet line for scannability.
21. **Name the specific person and company behind a source, not just the company.** E.g. "Thariq, on the Claude Code team at Anthropic" rather than just "Anthropic." A named individual's stated claim carries more credibility than an anonymous corporate voice, and it's a more honest attribution of whose words these actually are.
22. **Attribute with a short inline-hyperlinked phrase, not a named-and-quoted setup.** Superseded 2026-08-04 (Module 3 independent-edit comparison): instead of "The Claude Code Docs' [Page Name](url) page frames it directly: 'exact quote'," write a light inline-hyperlinked lead like "According to Anthropic, subagents help you:" or "Anthropic lays out four approaches side by side" (link on the phrase itself), then paraphrase close to the source. Drop the page-name-in-brackets convention and most direct quotation marks. Reserve verbatim quoting for the rare sentence that's genuinely the load-bearing substance of a named individual's argument (per rule 4) — don't wrap every quote in "he said" / "she argues" / "[Name] writes"; name the source once near the top to establish authority, then let quotes and prose speak for themselves.
23. **Preserve the source's own structure/framework when they have one** (e.g. a numbered "then vs. now" list, a named set of stages). Don't reorganize their argument into our own structure. If they present six points in an order, keep that order and those groupings.
24. **Do not add our own synthesis, commentary, action items, caveats, or "here's how this applies to you" sections.** This guide is curation only, not analysis on top of curation. Everything in a lesson should trace back to the source(s) being distilled; nothing should read as our own added judgment, audit checklist, or limitations section layered on top.
25. **Prefer weaving a quoted phrase into a sentence of running prose over pulling it into a standalone blockquote, even for short/definitional one-liners from a named individual.** Reserve an actual blockquote element for cases that genuinely need visual separation. Default to inline.
26. **When asked for alternative headline/heading options, give a short numbered list (4-5) and state a clear pick with a one-line reason, not just a bare list of options.**
27. **For a real chart/infographic in the source, flag it with a bracketed note** (e.g. `*[Image: ...]*`) suggesting where it could go — only when the source PDF actually contains that image, never as a "capture your own" suggestion. **Videos are not our job:** don't source, hunt, or bracket-suggest video content at all (superseded 2026-08-04, Module 3). The user embeds real video links/thumbnails themselves during their independent edit pass, with their own captions (e.g. "If you prefer to watch:").
28. **Module 1 is a beginner's on-ramp, not a practitioner reference: filter every source down to fundamentals only.** The reader has no prior agentic engineering knowledge. Most of our sources are written for practitioners and go deep fast; Module 1's job is to curate only the conceptual floor a newcomer needs, not everything the source covers. By the end of Module 1, the reader should feel confident and ready to build, not overwhelmed by depth they don't need yet. When a source's later sections go deep (implementation mechanics, edge cases, tuning knobs, API/config details), leave that material for the later module where it's already slated to appear (per the outline's per-lesson source notes) rather than including it here. This is the same filter already applied to 1.3 (context engineering) and 1.4 (tools); apply it to every remaining Module 1 lesson too.
29. **Don't give the module's closing recap its own numbered lesson.** Fold the "By this point you should have:" summary and the transition line to the next module straight onto the end of the last real lesson instead, unheaded (found 2026-08-06, Module 4 finished-version comparison — the draft's numbered "Recap" section was merged away in the user's edit pass).
30. **Paragraphs trend toward 1-2 sentences, not the 2-3 ceiling in rule 18.** Confirmed 2026-08-06 against the finished Module 4: the user's actual edit pass split paragraphs even shorter than rule 18's max, with a blank line between nearly every sentence pair. Default to one idea per paragraph; only combine two sentences when splitting them would feel choppy.
31. **Rewrite every lesson's top-level heading into full "How To [verb] X" phrasing, in Title Case, not just a colon-tagged benefit phrase.** Confirmed 2026-08-07 comparing draft vs. final Modules 1-4: "2.3 Giving the Agent Context: CLAUDE.md" became "2.3 How To Write The Best CLAUDE.md File"; "4.5 Run Claude Code Without a Terminal: Scripts and Schedules" became "4.5 How To Run Scripts And Schedules On Claude Code." Extends rule 13 (specific/benefit-oriented) with a concrete, consistently-applied phrasing template for the lesson-level heading specifically. Apply from first draft going forward, module index included.
32. **Never open a sentence with "Most."** Added 2026-09-04. "Most marketers open a chat window and type...", "Most teams struggle with...", "Most people don't realize..." are AI writing tics. The construction invents a statistic nobody measured, then uses it to set up a contrast. Rewrite so the reader is the subject and the claim is concrete: "Open a chat window, type X, and you get back Y." Applies equally to "Many," "Some," and "Every" used as a sentence-opening generalization about a population.
33. **Never use the negate-then-correct twin sentence.** Added 2026-09-04. The pattern is one sentence that denies something, followed by a near-identical sentence that supplies the replacement, usually with the subject repeated verbatim:
    - "The fix is not a better prompt. The fix is a better input."
    - "The difference is not prompt craft. The difference is that..."
    - "You do not need an interviewer. You need a transcript."
    - "A summary is not the goal. The goal is to..."

    State the positive claim on its own and let it stand. When the contrast genuinely carries meaning, fold it into one sentence as a subordinate clause ("Your job is to find it, not to add to it") instead of splitting it across two. The same rule governs the heading form "X Is The Y, Not The Z." It also covers the standalone negation opener ("The list is not the output"), which sets up the same rhythm even when no twin sentence follows.
34. **No "Tools Required" section.** Added 2026-09-04. Dropped from every lesson in Module 1 and not to be reintroduced anywhere in the resource. A table of tools with a "role in the workflow" column restates what the prose already said, pushes the reader's eye away from the method, and dates fast as tools change. Name each tool inline at the moment the practitioner actually uses it, linked per rule 19. Where a tool has a real prerequisite (a paid plan, a setting that must be switched on), state it as one sentence in the prose or as a step inside the hands-on section, not as a table row.
35. **Every lesson must link to the source article or episode itself, inline, on first attribution.** Added 2026-09-04 after a full-module audit found zero source links across eleven finished lessons. Linking the practitioner's own homepage or the publication's front page does NOT satisfy this; the link must reach the specific piece the lesson is distilled from. Per rules 5 and 22, weave it into the sentence as a descriptive phrase rather than appending a bare title or URL, e.g. "walked through it [on Marketing Against the Grain](url)" or "described it in [three fixes for AI slop in your marketing content](url)". Where a lesson draws on more than one source, link each one at the point its material first appears, not all together. This coexists with rule 6: still no per-lesson `**Source:**` line and no end-of-module Further Reading list.
36. **Never announce that something is important before saying it.** Added 2026-09-04. Sentences whose only job is to flag the significance of the next sentence are an AI tic. Banned forms include "X is the sentence the rest of the lesson rests on," "this is the key point," "here is the load-bearing claim," "the one rule their whole operation rests on," "that last part is what really matters," and any variant that builds a runway in front of a quote or a finding. Delete the runway and let the quote or finding land on its own; a reader who needs to be told a sentence is important will not believe it anyway. This extends rules 3 ("why this matters") and 12 ("worth noting") to cover the setup-before-a-quote version of the same move. Related tic to avoid: "which is what makes X work" tacked onto the end of a clause.
37. **Summarise the source. Do not rewrite it or synthesise on top of it.** Added 2026-09-04. Our job across the whole resource is curation: render what the practitioner said, more tightly, in their own order and their own terms. Two failure modes this bans:
    - **Rewriting.** Recasting the source's material into new prose of our own construction, so the sentence is ours and only the fact underneath is theirs. This most often happens under length pressure. When a lesson runs long, cut what the source did not say, tighten the summary, or drop a section. Do not paraphrase further and further from the source until the prose is ours.
    - **Synthesising.** Combining, extending, or concluding beyond what the source supports, including bridging two practitioners into a single argument neither of them made. Rule 24 already bans added commentary and action items; this extends it to added *reasoning*.

    The test: for every sentence, you can point at the passage in the source it summarises. A sentence you cannot trace is either cut or explicitly labelled as ours, the way our reconstructed prompts and hands-on sections already are.

    **This applies retroactively.** Module 1's compression pass rewrote roughly a third of its prose to hit the length target, before this rule existed. Its pending re-verification should audit for rule 37 specifically, not only for the tics, and the same check belongs on Module 2's trim pass.
